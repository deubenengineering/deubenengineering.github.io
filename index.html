01 / PREFACE

Do more. Have more. Be more.

It is easy to absorb the idea that happiness is something we earn by becoming a better version of ourselves: more successful, more attractive, more confident, more admired. There is always something to improve, something to acquire, some distance between who we are and who we think we should be.

And behind all that effort, a quieter question can linger: Am I enough yet?

Wanting a fulfilling life is natural. But when life becomes a constant performance, even our achievements can leave us uneasy. Approval feels reassuring until we fear losing it. Comparing ourselves with others gives us endless ways to fall short. We may become so occupied with how our lives appear that we lose touch with how they feel.

What happens if we turn the question around? Instead of asking how to become someone worthy of happiness, what if we ask how to meet the life we have with greater understanding and care? With its pleasures, uncertainties and pain.

02 / BEYOND THE PALACE WALLS

Beyond the Palace Walls

A traditional account of the Buddha’s early life tells of a young prince, Siddhartha, raised amid comfort and sheltered from suffering. Within the palace walls, his desires could be satisfied and the painful realities of life kept at a distance.

Then he ventured outside.

He encountered an old person, a sick person, and a dead body. Ageing, illness, and death were no longer distant ideas. They were realities that comfort and privilege could not keep away forever. A fourth encounter, with a spiritual seeker, revealed another possibility: a life devoted to understanding suffering and finding freedom from it.

We have our own versions of palace walls: staying busy, seeking reassurance, filling the next silence with entertainment. The wish to retreat is deeply understandable. Pleasure and comfort have a welcome place in our lives. Yet even the things we enjoy cannot protect us from every loss, disappointment, or fear.

Somewhere, we know this. The difficult conversation still waits. Someone we love is struggling. Our own uncertainty returns when the distractions grow quiet.

03 / TURNING TOWARD SUFFERING

“But I Want to Be Happy”

So what happens if we actually take the Buddha’s path and turn toward the suffering we would rather avoid?

Our first response might be: You must be crazy. I want to be happy. Why would I spend time looking at suffering? I want it to go away.

That objection deserves to be heard. When we are already overwhelmed, turning toward pain can sound like an invitation to feel worse.

But consider what happens when someone takes your distress seriously. They do not rush you, judge you, or insist that you look on the bright side. They stay long enough to understand. The problem may remain, yet you no longer have to carry it in quite the same way.

Compassion brings this willingness to our encounter with suffering: to recognize it, understand what may be causing it, and consider what could help. It asks for courage because paying attention can reveal things we cannot immediately fix.

It can also change the questions we ask.

Why am I so weak? can become What am I struggling with?

Why can’t they just get over it? can become What am I failing to understand?

Whose fault is this? can make room for What needs to happen now?

Responsibility still matters. Harm may need to be confronted, boundaries set, and difficult decisions made. Compassion asks us to approach those decisions with a commitment to reducing suffering.

04 / A WIDER POSSIBILITY

A Wider Possibility

This matters beyond our private lives. The values we bring to suffering shape how we raise children, build relationships, lead organizations, and respond to people whose lives differ from our own.

If we treat every struggle as a personal failure, we make it harder to ask for help. If we recognize vulnerability as part of being human, we create more room for honesty, responsibility, and support.

But what difference can my compassion make when the problems are this large?

We cannot know every consequence of the care we offer. We can recognize that our responses affect the people around us and that those encounters are part of the world we share.

Perhaps the question is worth carrying with us: What becomes possible when we spend less energy proving ourselves and more attention understanding what is needed?

Every moment is a place to begin.
